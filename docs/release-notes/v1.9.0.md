Clips that are the length you asked for, that open on the hook, and whose titles you can rewrite.

## The app can hear now

Picking the best moments used to be one opinion: a model reading a transcript. A transcript is words on a page. It does not contain the scream, the laugh, or the half-second of silence before the punchline — and two moments that read exactly the same can be worlds apart in the room.

So the app now listens to the video as well as reading it. It measures the whole source once and folds four things into the ranking:

- **How loud the moment is** against the rest of *that* video, so one noisy scene cannot make everything look like a hook
- **Reaction phrases** — *no way*, *wait for it*, *I can't believe* — counted double when they are the opening line
- **The quiet before the bang.** A still beat and then a spike reads as a payoff. A loud run-up to a loud moment reads as more noise
- **Dead air at the top.** Two seconds of near-silence at the start is the one thing that sinks a Short no matter how good second twenty is

How much all of that is allowed to move the ranking depends on how much of it could actually be measured, so a video with unreadable audio leans on the model instead of guessing loudly.

## 30 seconds now means 30 seconds

Ask for 30-second clips and you got 19, 24, 47. The model was doing arithmetic on timestamps while also writing a title and holding a format — it is the wrong job to give it.

Length is now worked out in code. Every clip is walked out to whole sentences until it lands in the length you asked for: extended toward the middle of that range when the model stopped short, and cut at the last sentence that fits when it ran long. Because it only ever moves in whole sentences, hitting the number never chops a word in half.

The same fix put the openings right. The model always named the line it wanted to open on, and it names that line accurately — it was the *timestamp* beside it that landed seconds early, on the throat-clear before it. The app now finds that line in the transcript and starts there. Clips that would have opened on two seconds of silence step forward to where the talking actually starts.

## The hook goes first, even when it happens later

Some moments are a build and then a payoff. Open on the build and the first second of your Short is someone talking quietly, which is the second you cannot afford to spend.

So a clip whose loudest moment lands late now opens with about two seconds of that moment, then plays in full. The viewer meets the payoff before deciding whether to stay, then watches it arrive properly.

It leaves alone any clip that already opens on its hook, where a replay would just be a stutter. And the room it takes is set aside *before* the cut is chosen, so a 30-second clip is still 30 seconds with it on the front. There is a switch in the Render panel, and you can write **no hook repeat** in the prompt.

Every export is also levelled to the loudness all three platforms mix toward. A Short that plays quieter than the one before it in the feed sounds cheap before a word of it is heard.

## Titles that name the thing

A title that does not say what the video is about is invisible to everyone who is not already subscribed — there is no word in it anyone would ever search for. The app used to ask the model to work that out per clip, from a transcript where nobody ever says the name of the game they are playing.

Now it works out what the video is about **once** — the game, the show, the person — and every clip is written around it, with one set of hashtags for the whole run instead of ten clips inventing ten ways to say the same thing.

## Your own title, kept

The title, description, tags and on-screen hook were already editable. They just were not saved — you would type a better title, close the panel, and find your words gone.

**Save changes** now keeps them, and renames the file on your PC to match the new title, so what is in the folder is what goes in YouTube's title box. Rewrite asks first if there is a title you wrote by hand.

## Show file works again

Clips are named after their own titles, and titles are full of commas. Windows Explorer was being handed the path as text it had to parse, and it split it at the first comma — so the button opened your Documents folder instead of the clip. It goes through the proper Windows call now and lands on the file, whatever it is called.
